Start with fuel: gas, electric or heat pump

The fuel you already have usually sets the easiest path. A gas heater needs a gas line, a flue and enough combustion air. An electric one needs a dedicated circuit and a disconnect, and no flue at all. Changing from one to the other is possible, but it is a project of its own.

Heat pump models deserve a short look if you have room. The U.S. Department of Energy says they move heat rather than make it, which can use far less electricity than a plain resistance heater.

They want a room that holds between 40 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit all year with about 1,000 cubic feet of air, and they chill the space, so a furnace room beats a cold garage.

Moving the heater or switching fuels

People relocate a heater to free up a closet, to get it closer to the bathrooms or to move it out of a damp spot.

Each move changes pipe runs, the vent path or the electrical feed, and every change adds parts and inspection steps. A move of a few feet is a different job from taking a heater from a basement to a garage.

A fuel switch is more involved still. Going from gas to electric means a new circuit and possibly a panel upgrade, plus a way to cap the old gas line.

Going from electric to gas needs a gas supply and a vent that did not exist. We walk the house, explain what the change will touch and put the whole price in one number before we begin.

Venting and combustion air for gas installs

A gas heater has to send its exhaust out of the house, and that path decides whether the install is simple. An old atmospheric vent has to match the new unit and slope correctly. A power-vented or sealed-combustion model runs through a wall in plastic pipe but needs power and a termination point outside.

Combustion air matters too. A heater in a tight closet with no air supply can burn poorly and spill exhaust back into the room. We check clearances, flue condition and air supply before we set anything. If we find a problem we cannot correct within the plumbing scope, we say so and explain who to call.

Expansion tank, relief valve and pressure

Water expands when it is heated. If your plumbing is a closed system, with a check valve or backflow preventer on the incoming line, that extra volume has nowhere to go and the pressure inside the pipes climbs. A small expansion tank on the cold side absorbs it.

Whether you need one depends on your system, so we measure house pressure with a gauge during the visit. Repeated relief valve drips, banging pipes or a leaking fixture can all trace back to high pressure. The relief valve is the last safety device on a heater, so its discharge line is run to a safe spot and never capped.

Code, licensing and permit questions

The state plumbing subcode is the National Standard Plumbing Code Illustrated, 2021 edition, adopted at N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, and it governs how we vent, pipe and relieve a new heater.

Only a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor or their W-2 staff may do the work. Any plumber should hand you a license number, and the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers at njconsumeraffairs.gov/plu will confirm it.

Permit rules vary by the kind of work, so we do not guess. Winslow's Construction Office decides, and you can reach it at 609-567-0700 option 5 or by email. It is open 8:30 to 4:30, with plumbing inspections Tuesday to Thursday from 8am to 1pm, which is worth knowing when you pick a date.
