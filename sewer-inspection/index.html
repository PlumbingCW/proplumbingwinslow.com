Who needs a camera inspection, and who does not

Not every drain problem calls for one. A single slow bathroom sink does not. A line that has needed clearing three times in a year does, and so does a house you are about to buy.

Repeat clogs

If a cable opens the line and the clog returns, something in the pipe is causing it. The camera finds it.

Buying a home

A line that looks fine at the toilet can be cracked or full of roots below the lawn. An inspection before closing avoids an unwelcome surprise.

Sewer smell or wet spots

Odors in a basement or soft ground in the yard can come from a broken line. Footage tells you where.

Before a repair or jetting

We look first so we know whether a pipe can take the pressure.

We can also trace the camera signal from above ground to mark where the line runs and how deep it sits. That locate is how you know where a repair would have to happen, and it is a separate question from what the footage shows.

How the inspection goes

1

Find an entry point

We open a cleanout when the house has one. If not, a fixture such as a toilet may have to be pulled.

2

Run the camera

The head moves along the pipe while the monitor shows the picture and the distance counter.

3

Mark and note

Problem spots are recorded by distance and marked on the surface when a locate is needed.

4

Review it with you

We walk through the footage in plain terms and tell you what needs action and what does not.

What an inspection will not tell you

A camera shows the pipe's inside, not the soil around it and not the shape of a line that is full of water. It cannot see past a full clog, and we may need to clear the line first. If a house is on septic, a camera can look at the house line but not the tank or field, which belong to a septic contractor.
