Water heater leaking: tank, fitting, relief valve or condensation

Where the water comes from decides whether you need a repair or a new heater. Wipe the area dry, put a dry paper towel under the suspect spot, and check it again in a few minutes. The towel will show you whether the drip is steady, intermittent or only appears when the burner or element is running.

Tank body

Water seeping from the bottom or the seam of the tank jacket usually means the steel tank has corroded through. No part swap fixes that, so replacement is the honest answer.

Fittings and connections

Drips at the cold inlet, hot outlet, drain valve or a flex connector are often repairable. A tightened union, a new valve or a fresh connector can end the leak.

Temperature and pressure relief valve

A valve that drips, or a discharge pipe that runs, is reporting excess pressure or heat. Never cap it. We test the valve and look for the pressure problem behind it.

Condensation and drip pans

High-efficiency gas units can produce condensate, and cold pipes sweat in humid weather. Wet that dries up on its own is not always a leak, but a pan that keeps filling needs a look.

The Township notes that a higher-than-normal water bill can point to an undetected leak. A slow drip at a heater fitting in a garage or utility closet can run for weeks before anyone sees a puddle. If your bill has crept up and the heater sits on a concrete floor that always looks a little damp, have it checked.

Gas smell? Do not wait for a plumber

If you smell gas near a gas-fired heater, leave the building, then call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or relight a pilot. A plumber comes after the building is safe.

No hot water, or water that runs lukewarm

When there is no hot water, the cause depends on the fuel. On an electric heater the usual suspects are a tripped breaker, a failed heating element or a bad thermostat. On a gas heater the pilot, igniter, flame sensor or gas control valve is the first place to look.

Lukewarm water, or hot water that runs out quickly, points in a different direction. A failed lower element, a long layer of sediment at the bottom of the tank, or a damaged dip tube can all shorten the supply. We do not guess at these from the phone, because the fix for each is very different.

  • ✓Check that the breaker for an electric heater has not tripped. Reset it once only.
  • ✓Look at the gas heater's pilot window or display, and read any error code off the label.
  • ✓Note whether the cold water at your faucets is normal, since that rules out a supply problem.
  • ✓Keep the hot tap closed and the heater's relief valve alone. Do not drain a tank that is not leaking.

Repair or replace: how we decide on the spot

The decision rests on three things: what failed, how old the unit is, and what the repair would cost compared with a new heater. A thermostat or element on a heater in decent shape is a sensible repair. The same part on a tank that is badly corroded inside is money spent on a unit that is near the end.

The data plate on the side of the tank carries the model and serial numbers, and the manufacturer's website can decode the build date from the serial number. We read that plate and tell you what we find. We do not quote a lifespan from memory, because it varies by brand, fuel and water.

Repair tends to make sense when

A single part has failed, the tank is dry and sound, the heater is not old for its type, and the fix is a small share of a new unit.

Replacement tends to make sense when

The tank leaks, rust shows around the base, the same heater has needed repeat repairs, or the parts cost close to a new unit.
