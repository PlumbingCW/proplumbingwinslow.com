Stop the flood before anything else

Water rising in the bowl means a blockage, and flushing again only adds more. Shut the supply valve, then wait. If the water is dark, smells like sewage, or other drains in the house are gurgling, stop using every drain and call, because the problem may be farther down the line.

Toilet leaking, overflowing or running: sort out which one

Overflowing bowl

Usually a clog in the trap or the drain behind it. The fixture can often be cleared the same visit. A repeat overflow points to the main line.

Water around the base

The wax ring or closet bolts are the first suspects. A rocking toilet breaks the seal, and the leak runs into the subfloor.

Running after the flush

A flapper that does not seat, a fill valve that never shuts off, or a float set too high.

Leak from the tank

A tank bolt gasket, a cracked tank, or a loose supply connection. Condensation on a cold tank looks like a leak but is not.

Weak or slow flush

Mineral buildup in the rim holes, a partly blocked trap, or a flapper that closes early.

Many of these can be told apart with a dry paper towel. Wipe the tank, the supply line and the base, then check for fresh wetness after fifteen minutes. A towel that stays dry under the tank and wet only at the floor tells the plumber where to start.

A running toilet costs more than it sounds

A toilet that keeps trickling after the flush is the quietest water leak in a house. The Township notes that a higher water bill can point to an undetected leak, and toilets and shower heads are the places it names.

In homes on the Township system in Sicklerville, 08081, or Cedar Brook, 08018, a bill that rises without a change in habits is a good reason to check the toilets first.

To test one, drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait a quarter of an hour without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking. If the bill stays high after that, a leak detection visit looks for water escaping somewhere you cannot see.

Wax rings and why a rocking toilet matters

The wax ring is a donut of sealant between the toilet and the floor flange. It is out of sight, which is why a bad one often goes unnoticed until the floor softens or a stain shows on a ceiling below.

If the toilet moves when you sit, or you see water at the base after flushing, the seal is probably broken.

Replacing the ring means lifting the toilet, checking the flange and the subfloor, and setting the toilet back level. A flange sitting too low or a rotted floor needs more work than a new ring. We would rather tell you that on arrival than set the toilet on a floor that will fail.

Fill valves, flappers and handles

The parts inside the tank are cheap, and they wear out on about the same schedule. A fill valve that hisses, a handle that must be held down, and a flapper that has gone soft are all signs. Replacing them together is usually better than doing one at a time, since the labor is the same visit.

Installing a new toilet in Winslow, NJ

A toilet installation starts with shutting the water, draining the old unit, and pulling it from the flange. The plumber inspects the flange, installs a new seal and supply line, sets the new toilet level, and tests for leaks after several flushes.

Removal and disposal of the old toilet can be included, so ask for it to be listed in the price.

If you are adding a toilet where none existed, the work becomes new plumbing with drains, vents and supply, and falls under plumbing installation. Permit rules vary by the work, and the Construction Office at 609-567-0700, option 5, decides.

In Winslow Crossing and other 1970s Sicklerville neighborhoods, replacing a toilet is usually a like-for-like swap, but a bathroom redo can open walls where older shutoffs and drains turn up.
