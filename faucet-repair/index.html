A leaking faucet or valve that cannot wait

Most faucet trouble starts as a slow drip and stays boring for months. The urgent version is different: water spraying from the handle, a stem that will not seal, or a supply line dripping onto a cabinet floor.

Cabinet bases are made of pressed wood that swells and crumbles once it is wet, so a small leak under a vanity can turn into a larger repair.

While you wait for the plumber, clear the cabinet, set a bucket or towel under the drip, and keep the water off at the fixture valve.

Do not use a wrench on a valve that is crusted over, because a stuck stem can snap and make the leak worse. If water is near an outlet or a power strip, switch off that circuit at the panel first.

Faucet, valve and fixture repair, step by step

A repair visit starts with water off at the fixture and a look at the model. Many modern faucets use a sealed cartridge, so the fix is a swap rather than a rebuild, and the plumber can tell quickly whether the part is still sold.

An older single-handle faucet with pitted metal is a different story, since a new cartridge in a corroded body often leaks again within a season.

When a supply valve fails or will not close

The small stop valves under a sink or behind a toilet are the part everyone forgets until one fails. Old multi-turn valves can seep from the stem, and some will not close at all after years without being moved.

If you cannot isolate a single fixture, the whole house has to go off at the main every time someone works on it.

That is why replacing a seized or dripping shutoff is worth doing during the same visit. If a valve bursts or cracks in the wall, close the main shutoff and call at once, day or night.

Shower valves, showerheads and tub faucets

A showerhead that drips after you turn it off is often a worn cartridge or a bad seat behind the wall plate, not the head itself.

A new head fixes low flow caused by mineral clogging, but it will not stop a drip. Water that appears on a ceiling below a shower deserves a prompt visit, since a leaking valve body is hidden behind tile.

Bathtub faucets have their own pattern. A spout that dribbles after the diverter is lifted usually needs a new diverter, and a tub filler that leaks around the handle needs seals or a cartridge. If reaching the valve means opening a wall, the plumber explains the opening before any cutting starts.
